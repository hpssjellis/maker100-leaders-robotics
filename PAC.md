# Bring Maker100 Leaders Robotics to Your High School
## A Free, Open-Source Course — Piloted for About $2,000

## The Big Idea First

**Maker100 Leaders Robotics is free, open source, and permanently online.** It isn't a product a school buys once — it's a living curriculum that keeps evolving as hardware gets cheaper and better, and it will still be there next year, and the year after. The only real cost is the hardware students build with.

This proposal asks the PAC to fund a **small pilot — about $2,000 for 7 students** — inside a course that already exists at the school. But **7 is just a doable starting number, not a ceiling.** A teacher confident enough could run this with 15 students, or an entire class. Seven is simply a low-risk size to prove it out first.

## What This Actually Prepares Students For

This is the part that matters more than any logistics below:

- **It prepares leaders for a world that keeps changing.** The hardware is obsolete in a year or two, on purpose — students learn to adapt to new tools, not memorize one kit.
- **It prepares engineers for hands-on technology before the math of university.** Students hit real physical failure — a bad connection, a sensor that lies — long before they hit the theory that explains why, which makes the theory click harder when it finally arrives.
- **It prepares makers for the power of the modern world.** Working hardware, real sensors, and AI as a tool rather than a black box — students leave understanding how the systems around them actually work, not just how to consume them.

## No Specialist Required — Not Even the Teacher

- **Students do the problem-solving.** The host teacher's job is safety, structure, and encouragement — not knowing every answer.
- **The Analog Firewall**: students hand-write summaries and draw circuit diagrams before touching code, forcing real understanding instead of copy-paste assembly.
- **Teach Three**: whoever solves a problem first teaches three others, who teach three more — knowledge moves student-to-student.
- **LLMs (ChatGPT or private local models)** are used as problem-solving aids, the way engineers actually use them — but the ~50 physical assignments still have to work before students move to final projects.
- **I'm available as a resource, not a requirement.** I can TOC (teacher-on-call) for up to three sessions a semester — at startup, when students hit a genuinely stuck point, and for final-project help — but the course is built so I'm not needed. Any Arduino-comfortable maker, teacher, or parent volunteer can fill that same role. The curriculum doesn't depend on me.

## Why the PAC Should Want This

Funding this pilot is a visible, low-cost way to say: *we invest in students who want to build things, not just consume technology.* Because the curriculum itself is free and permanent, the PAC's money buys hardware and a proof point — not a product that expires or a program that needs re-funding to keep existing.

## The PAC Ask

1. Approve pilot funding (~$2,000 USD for 7 students, per the 2026 price list — adjustable for a different group size)
2. Identify one existing course and teacher willing to host the pilot
3. Confirm IT support for installing required software (Arduino IDE, Python, Node.js, USB/serial access)
4. Treat this as a **test**, not a commitment — the goal is real evidence, not a promise

## An Important Note on Funding Beyond the Pilot

The PAC is well positioned to fund this **pilot** — a small, one-time cost. It is *not* the right long-term funder for a full course, and it shouldn't need to be, since the curriculum itself is free and ongoing. If the pilot succeeds, expanding to a full course means more hardware — funded by the school/district — not a new curriculum cost. **The PAC's role is to buy the evidence, not to become a permanent funding source.**

## Cons & Potential Hurdles (Things to Anticipate)

**Classroom management & noise.** Students heads-down on breadboards and code tend to be quiet and focused. The more realistic risk is the *other* students in the room being curious or distracted — worth a quick conversation with the host teacher up front.

**IT department friction.** Installing the Arduino IDE is normally routine. If USB/serial permissions hit friction, **PlatformIO with VS Code** is a solid fallback.

**Assessment complexity.** Students track their own progress against a **shared chart of all ~50 assignments**, checked off as each is physically completed and verified — lightweight for the teacher, still auditable.

**Selection.** Students are selected using **combined math/science grades**, plus priority for anyone with **prior Raspberry Pi or Arduino experience, or stated engineering aspirations**. As the pilot grows into a full class, the original cohort's job becomes **teaching the incoming students** — Teach Three at full-class scale.

# Appendix: Common Questions

**Is this really free?**
Yes — the curriculum is open source on GitHub and stays that way. The pilot cost is hardware, not licensing or curriculum fees.

**Does it have to be exactly 7 students?**
No. Seven is a low-risk number to pilot with. A confident teacher could run this with a full class from day one.

**Do you have to be there for it to run?**
No. I can TOC up to three times a semester for startup, mid-course, and final-project support, but the course is designed so any Arduino-comfortable adult can fill that role, or none at all once the teacher has run it once.

**Does the teacher have to teach seven students separately?**
No. They remain part of the existing class, working Maker100 during an agreed slice of class time.

**Won't students just get ChatGPT to do everything?**
Physical hardware, handwritten planning, and peer teaching make pure copy-paste ineffective — the code still has to make real hardware work.

**What if the technology changes?**
Expected and fine — the curriculum emphasizes concepts and connections, not one fixed generation of hardware.

## Suggested Pilot Steps

1. **Identify a host course** and a willing teacher.
2. **Select ~7 students** (or more) by combined math/science grade, prioritizing prior Raspberry Pi/Arduino experience or engineering aspirations.
3. **Agree on logistics** — class time, progress tracking via the 50-assignment chart.
4. **Prep the tech** — hardware, software install, USB/serial permissions.
5. **Run the pilot** — Build → Test → Fail → Understand → Fix → Explain.
6. **Measure results** — completed projects, independence, teacher workload, interest in continuing.
7. **Decide** — expand, adjust, or stop. Since the curriculum is free, expansion only costs hardware.
