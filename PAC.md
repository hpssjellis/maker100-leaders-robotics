# Bring Maker100 Leaders Robotics to Your High School
## A $2,000, 7 Student PAC Pilot Proposal

## The Idea

Give a small group of motivated high school students the chance to explore **robotics, electronics, programming, AI, physics, and engineering** through a hands-on, project-based course — without asking the school to create a new standalone class.

**Maker100 Leaders Robotics** is a meta-course embedded inside an *existing* computer lab, robotics, electronics, programming, or web-design course. The 7 (±1) students aren't a new class — they're selected from a class that already exists, with a teacher who already teaches it. For example: a Grade 10 Web Design class has 25 students. Seven motivated students run Maker100 projects during an agreed portion of class time, while the teacher continues teaching the rest of the class as normal.

## Why the PAC Should Want This

This isn't just "robotics club" — it's a low-cost way to give students a taste of real engineering practice before they leave for post-secondary or the workforce: working with unfamiliar hardware, using AI tools the way professionals actually do, documenting and defending their own reasoning, and teaching what they learn to others. Those are the skills colleges and employers say they can't find enough of.

For a PAC, funding this pilot is a visible, concrete way to say: *we invest in students who want to build things, not just consume technology.* It costs a fraction of what most enrichment programs cost, it doesn't commit the school to anything ongoing, and if it works, the PAC gets to point to real students and real projects as proof that the investment mattered — rather than a one-off assembly or a piece of equipment that sits unused.

## Why No Robotics Specialist Is Required

- **Students do the problem-solving.** The host teacher's job is safety, structure, and guidance — not knowing every answer.
- **Hardware changes fast, on purpose** — it keeps generating new problems to solve instead of going stale, and the core concepts (inputs, sensors, outputs, feedback, debugging) stay stable even as boards get cheaper and faster.
- **The Analog Firewall**: students hand-write summaries and draw circuit diagrams before touching code, forcing real understanding instead of copy-paste assembly.
- **Teach Three**: whoever makes a discovery passes it on to three other students — knowledge moves student-to-student, not just teacher-to-student.
- **LLMs (ChatGPT or private local models)** are used as problem-solving aids, the way they're used in real engineering — but the ~50 physical assignments still have to actually work before students move to final projects.
- **Interconnected/bundled hardware kits are avoided** — they tend to hide the connections between components (sensor → data → program → decision → actuator), and those connections are a big part of the learning.

## The PAC Ask

1. Approve pilot funding (~$2,000 USD for 7 students, per the 2026 price list)
2. Identify one existing course and teacher willing to host the pilot
3. Confirm IT support for installing required software (Arduino IDE, Python, Node.js, USB/serial access)
4. Treat this as a **test**, not a commitment — the goal is real evidence, not a promise

## An Important Note on Funding Beyond the Pilot

The PAC is well positioned to fund this **pilot** — a small, one-time cost to prove the concept. It is *not* the right long-term funder for a full standalone course. If the pilot succeeds, the decision to run Maker100 as a full course — and its ongoing funding — should shift to the administration/district budget. **The PAC's role is to buy the evidence, not to become the permanent funding source.**

## Cons & Potential Hurdles (Things to Anticipate)

**Classroom management & noise.** Students heads-down on breadboards and code tend to be quiet and focused. The more realistic risk is the *other* students in the room being curious or distracted by the hands-on work happening nearby — worth a quick conversation with the host teacher up front.

**IT department friction.** Installing the Arduino IDE is normally a routine, low-risk request. If USB/serial permissions do hit friction, **PlatformIO with VS Code** is a solid fallback that installs cleanly on Windows.

**Assessment complexity.** Rather than asking the host teacher to build a parallel grading system while teaching a different subject, students track their own progress against a **shared chart of all ~50 assignments**, checked off as each is physically completed and verified. Lightweight for the teacher, still an auditable record of progress.

**Selection.** Students are selected using **combined math/science grades**, plus priority for anyone with **prior Raspberry Pi or Arduino experience, or stated engineering aspirations** — a straightforward, defensible basis rather than teacher subjective pick. Because this is a small proof-of-concept, it's deliberately scoped to students who can work independently with minimal support. When the program expands to a full class, the pilot cohort's role shifts to **teaching the incoming students** — Teach Three at full-class scale — which is how the model extends access rather than staying limited to an initial small group.

# Appendix: Common Questions

**Does the teacher have to teach seven students separately?**
No. The seven remain part of the existing class, working Maker100 during an agreed slice of class time while the teacher supports everyone else as normal.

**Won't students just get ChatGPT to do everything?**
Physical hardware, handwritten planning, and peer teaching make pure copy-paste ineffective — the code still has to make real hardware work.

**What if the technology changes?**
Expected and fine — the curriculum emphasizes concepts and connections, not one fixed generation of hardware.

## Suggested Pilot Steps

1. **Identify a host course** and a willing teacher.
2. **Select ~7 students** (±1) by combined math/science grade, prioritizing prior Raspberry Pi/Arduino experience or engineering aspirations.
3. **Agree on logistics** — class time, progress tracking via the 50-assignment chart, normal course requirements.
4. **Prep the tech** — hardware, software install (Arduino IDE, PlatformIO/VS Code as fallback), USB/serial permissions.
5. **Run the pilot** — Build → Test → Fail → Understand → Fix → Explain.
6. **Measure results** — completed projects, independence, teacher workload, interest in continuing.
7. **Decide** — expand, adjust, or stop. If expanding, hand funding to the administration and have pilot students help teach the incoming class.
